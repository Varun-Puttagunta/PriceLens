Methodology

How the number is built.

A valuation you cannot interrogate is a guess with better typography. This is the whole pipeline — what it measures, how it weighs evidence, where its error comes from, and the questions it is not equipped to answer.

1. Fitting the market

Before a single comparable is chosen, the engine fits a hedonic regression across every closed sale it can see. The dependent variable is log(sale price); the regressors are the features of the property, the month it closed, and dummy variables for submarket and property type.

log(price) = β₀ + β₁·month + β₂·log(sqft) + β₃·beds + β₄·baths + β₅·garage + β₆·lot + β₇·decade + β₈·condition + β₉·pool + β₁₀·greenbelt + submarket dummies + type dummies

This does two jobs. The coefficient on month is the market's drift with composition held still — which matters, because a quarter that happened to be heavy on new-build Prosper closings shows up as appreciation in a raw price-per-square-foot series and doesn't here.

The rest of the coefficients are the adjustment rates. This is the part most comparative market analyses get wrong: they carry a rule of thumb in from somewhere else — "a bathroom is worth ten thousand dollars" — and apply it to a market that never said so. PriceLens measures what this market pays, in this window, every time it runs.

Each rate is bounded to a range an appraiser would sign off on, so one strange quarter cannot produce an absurd adjustment. A rate the data cannot separate from the others — bedroom count, usually, because it moves with square footage — is dropped rather than guessed at. Solved by ridge-regularised least squares, so collinear features degrade gracefully instead of exploding.

2. Choosing comparables

Every closed sale that recorded on or before the valuation date is scored for comparability against the subject, on a 0–1 scale:

score = 0.25·location + 0.22·size + 0.17·recency + 0.11·rooms + 0.10·type + 0.08·vintage + 0.07·submarket

Each component is a Gaussian decay rather than a threshold — a comp four-tenths of a mile away is not "in bounds" so much as slightly better than one at six-tenths. Distance is great-circle from the subject's coordinates; recency decays with a roughly five-month half-life.

The search runs in tiers, and starts deliberately tight: a mile and a half, six months, within 20% on size. Only if that grid returns fewer than three usable sales does it widen — to three miles, then six, then twelve. Which tier the valuation ended up on is reported, because a comp set that needed the twelve-mile net is telling you something.

3. The adjustment grid

Each comparable is first pulled forward to today at the fitted market rate, then walked toward the subject one feature at a time. The question every line answers is: what would this house have sold for, if it were your house?

Adjustments are computed in log space and reported in dollars:

adjustment = timeAdjustedPrice × (exp(βfeature × Δfeature) − 1)

That detail matters more than it looks. The obvious alternative — a flat dollar rate per square foot — carries real error across a large size gap, because price per square foot itself falls as homes get bigger. A 2,200 sqft comp adjusted to a 3,300 sqft subject at a flat rate overshoots badly. The log form has the elasticity built in.

Two figures are tracked for every comp: the net adjustment (the sum) and the gross adjustment (the sum of absolute values). A comp whose gross adjustment exceeds 25% of its price is flagged and down-weighted. It has been argued into place rather than actually being comparable.

4. Reconciliation

The adjusted comps are combined into one estimate by weighted average, where the weight rewards genuine comparability and punishes heavy adjustment:

weight = similarity^2.2 × exp(−grossAdjustment / 0.15)

The exponent on similarity concentrates the estimate on the closest matches instead of letting six mediocre comps outvote one good one. The exponential penalty means a comp needing 30% gross adjustment contributes roughly a third of what an 8% comp does.

The spread of the adjusted comps around that weighted mean becomes the range. A half-width is built from the dispersion plus a floor plus a confidence term, and it is deliberately not tight: the coefficients were set so that roughly four sales in five land inside the stated band. A narrower range would read better and be a lie.

5. The confidence grade

Five signals are scored 0–100 and weighted into a single grade, so a seller can read the model's own opinion of its work without a statistics background.

SignalWeightWhat it catches
Agreement26%Comps that disagree with each other
Comparability24%Comps that aren't really like the subject
Comp count18%Thin evidence
Adjustment load18%Comps argued into place
Recency14%A comp set that has gone stale

Grades run A (82+), B (68+), C (52+) and D below that. A D is not a failure state — it is the model saying the evidence is thin, which is the most useful thing it can tell you about an unusual property.

6. From value to asking price

Value and list price are different questions, and the second one is the one sellers actually have to decide. The strategist sweeps candidate prices from 4% below the estimate to 10% above, and for each one projects the whole outcome.

Time on market

days = baseDom × exp(6.0 × over) + 2.6 × over%

Two terms, because a purely multiplicative model lets a fast market shrug off overpricing: in a twelve-day market, asking 5% high would read as four extra days. It does not work like that. An overpriced listing misses the new-listing surge and then waits for the market to appreciate into its number, and that wait is measured in absolute days regardless of how quickly correctly-priced homes are moving.

What it eventually sells for

premium(days) = 1.025 − 0.075 × (1 − exp(−days / 45)) expectedSale = min( value × premium(days) × (1 − 0.013 × cuts), listPrice × (1 + 0.035 × exp(−days / 25)) )

A listing in its first week carries a small competition premium; by month three the leverage has crossed the table. Each public price cut costs more than the cut itself, because it tells every watching buyer the seller is negotiable. And the second term is what makes underpricing expensive — you cannot sell far above your own asking price, so the ceiling comes down with the list.

Net proceeds

net = expectedSale − commission − carry × (days + daysToClose)

The recommendation is simply the price that maximises this. It is regularly not the highest number on the ladder, which is the entire point.

The search band

One last adjustment, and it is not a modelling nicety — it is how buyers actually behave. People filter listing portals in round steps: under $600K, under $625K. A house asking $627,000 is invisible to every buyer whose ceiling is $625,000, and those buyers were the closest ones. When the recommended price sits just over a band edge, it is dropped below it. The cost is a few hundred dollars of asking price; the gain is an entire audience.

7. How it is validated

Every closed listing in the book is re-priced using only sales that had already recorded on the day it went on the market, with the property itself excluded from its own comp set. That backtest runs live in your browser on the results page — it is not a number typed into a slide.

The engine also ships a test suite that runs in CI: it plants a known trend in a synthetic market and checks the regression recovers it, asserts that no comparable dated after its subject's listing date can reach the estimate, checks adjustment signs in both directions, and fails the build if backtest accuracy or range coverage regresses past the numbers published on this site.

About the data

The engagement figures are real: 36 properties and $21.8M in sales volume, over roughly twenty-one months.

The individual records on this site are not. Every address, coordinate and transaction in the published dataset is synthetic, generated from a hedonic model of a North Dallas market — client transactions are not published. The generator's parameters and the engine's are deliberately different, so the backtest measures the engine recovering a market it was not handed, which is the only version of that test worth running.

The portfolio totals are calibrated to the real headline figures, so the top line on the results page reads true. Everything else on that page is computed from the published data by the same code that runs the pricing tool — you can download it as CSV and check.

What it can't do

Being clear about this is part of the product, not a disclaimer bolted to the end of it.

  • It is not an appraisal. No one has been inside the house. Condition is a number someone typed, and a kitchen photographed well is not a kitchen renovated well.
  • It cannot see what isn't in the data. A power line behind the fence, a school boundary that moved, a foundation issue disclosed at inspection — all invisible, all worth more than most of the adjustments on the grid.
  • It assumes tomorrow resembles last quarter. The trend term extrapolates recent drift. At a genuine inflection — a rate shock, a major employer leaving — it will be confidently wrong, and the confidence grade will not warn you, because the comps still agree with each other.
  • Unusual properties break it. Acreage, historic homes, anything without six real neighbours. The model reports this honestly as a low grade and a wide range, but a wide range is not the same as a useful answer.
  • The adoption comparison is not a controlled experiment. The two groups are consecutive in time, not randomised. Some of the improvement is the tool; some is everything else that changed over those months. Thirteen listings against twenty-three is enough to act on, not enough to publish.
  • The demand model is calibrated, not derived. The elasticity and decay constants in the list-price model are fit to this market's behaviour. They should be re-fit anywhere else before anyone leans on the output.
